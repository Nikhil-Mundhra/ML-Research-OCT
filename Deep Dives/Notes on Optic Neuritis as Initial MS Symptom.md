# Notes on Optic Neuritis as the Initial Symptom of Multiple Sclerosis

Prepared October 9, 2026. Figures below were checked against primary papers, registries, or society publications, items we could not verify are flagged.

## Bottom line

Optic neuritis (ON) is the first symptom in roughly **1 in 4 people with relapsing onset MS, with a best supported range of about 20 to 25%**. The figure drops sharply in progressive onset MS (about 2%) and in older onset MS (about 6% over age 50 in one classic cohort). It climbs to roughly 28% if any visual presenting symptom is counted, and some referral cohorts report much higher values (31% to 47% in Iranian series). Over the whole disease course, ON affects a much larger share of patients, often quoted as about 50%.

So a defensible one line statement is: *optic neuritis is the first manifestation of MS in about one fifth to one quarter of patients.*

## Three different questions that get blended together

Much of the confusion in the literature comes from reporting different quantities under one label.

| Question | Typical value | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Isolated ON as the first clinical event, relapsing onset MS | 21 to 26% | The headline number |
| Any visual symptom at presentation | about 28% | Population based study in which ON accounted for 22% |
| ON at any point in the disease course | 27 to 66% in prior literature | Wide range reflects design and ancestry |
| Of people with a first ON, share who later develop MS | about 50% at 15 years | Reverse direction, from the ONTT |

The last row is a different statistic from the others. It answers "if I have ON, what is my MS risk?", not "if I have MS, how likely was ON to be first?".

## Evidence base

| Source | Design and setting | Sample | Finding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pleșa et al., Diagnostics 2024 | Longitudinal single center, Bucharest, patients on treatment | 127 | 37% had ON at some point; abstract reports 25.53% with ON as the initial symptom |
| Large European natural history table (2,054 patients) | Long term cohort | 2,054 | Isolated ON first in 21.5% overall, 26.2% of relapsing onset, 1.9% of progressive onset |
| London, Ontario natural history cohort (Weinshenker et al., 1989, as tabulated in McAlpine's Multiple Sclerosis) | Population referral cohort | 1,096 | ON first in 23% of those with onset before age 30, falling with age |
| Population based ocular manifestations study | Population based | Not stated in the summary I accessed | 28% had visual symptoms first, 22% as ON |
| Brazilian clinical cohort (UFMG) | Clinical cohort of definite MS | 1,030 | 17% had ON as the initial manifestation |
| Polish single center series | Single center | Small | ON first in about 20% |
| Saudi Arabian hospital series | Hospital based | Not stated in the summary | ON first in 27.9% |
| Northern Iran series | Hospital based | 263 | ON first in 46.8% |
| Isfahan, Iran | Population based, early onset focus | Not stated | ON first in 31% of early onset MS |

The prior literature range cited by the population based authors for ON as a presenting symptom is 13% to 31%, which brackets most of the values above except the northern Iran series.

### A note on the Pleșa et al. number

The published abstract states that 25.53% of patients had ON as the initial MS symptom and, separately, that 25.53% had residual visual acuity worse than 20/40. The same value appearing for two different outcomes is a flag. If 30 of 127 patients presented with ON, the rate is 23.6%. The value 25.53% is exactly 12 of 47, which suggests the percentage may have been calculated within the ON subgroup for at least one outcome. Check the full text tables before citing it. The study is useful as a regional data point but should not anchor the estimate: it is a single center, has 127 patients, enrolled people on disease modifying therapy, and relies partly on history and records for events that happened years earlier.

## How the figure changes with age and onset type

The London, Ontario data show a steady decline in ON as the first symptom with age at onset.

| Age at MS onset | ON as first symptom |
| --- | --- |
| Under 20 | 23% |
| 20 to 29 | 23% |
| 30 to 39 | 13% |
| 40 to 49 | 9% |
| 50 and over | 6% |

Sensory symptoms were the most common first symptom across all age bands in that cohort (32 to 52%), and insidious motor onset dominated after age 40. This matches the very low ON rate in progressive onset MS (about 2%) in the larger European table.

Two cautions apply. First, a late onset series from East Azerbaijan, Iran reported ON in 23% of late onset cases, well above the 6% historical figure, so regional and ascertainment differences matter. Second, I could not confirm in primary sources the claim, made in the document you shared, that pediatric onset ON rates are 30% to 44% across series. One small early onset series reported 35.8%, which is consistent with the claim but too small to settle it.

## Not every first ON is MS

A first episode of demyelinating ON can belong to several diseases. In the Acute Optic Neuritis Network (ACON), a prospective global study of 160 patients with a first ever acute ON suggestive of a demyelinating disease, 49% had single isolated ON, 26% had MS, 16% had MOG antibody associated disease, and 9% had neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder. The authors also note that antibody mediated ON is more common in people of Asian or African descent and that ON incidence worldwide is about 1 to 3 per 100,000 per year.

This matters for interpretation. Regional differences in the "ON first" rate partly reflect differences in the underlying disease mix and referral patterns, not only MS biology.

## From a first ON to an MS diagnosis

The Optic Neuritis Treatment Trial (ONTT) followed participants for 15 years. The cumulative probability of MS was 50% (95% CI 44% to 56%). It was 25% for those with no brain MRI lesions at baseline and 72% for those with one or more lesions. Among patients without baseline lesions, male sex, optic disc swelling, and atypical clinical features were associated with a substantially lower risk. After 10 years, the risk for those without lesions was very low.

A 2024 German study applying the 2017 McDonald criteria found that the classic triad of visual loss, color vision loss, and eye pain was present in only 47% of patients with MS related ON. Among them, 56% had a relative afferent pupillary defect, 61% had optic nerve abnormalities on MRI, and 81% had abnormal visual evoked potentials. Typical features are common but not universal.

## Diagnostic consequences: the 2024 McDonald revision

The 2024 revision of the McDonald criteria, published in The Lancet Neurology, adds the optic nerve as a fifth location for demonstrating dissemination in space, alongside periventricular, cortical or juxtacortical, infratentorial, and spinal cord regions. Dissemination in space can now be met with typical lesions in at least two of the five locations. Optic nerve involvement can be shown by MRI, OCT, or visual evoked potentials, provided there is no better explanation.

The consensus recommendations on OCT state that inter eye differences of 6 micrometers or more in peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer, or 4 micrometers or more in the combined macular ganglion cell and inner plexiform layer, support optic nerve injury. Delayed VEP latency can also support demyelinating injury, but it depends on the technique and the center.

Other changes in the same revision include relaxing the requirement for dissemination in time in certain scenarios and accepting the kappa free light chain index as an alternative to oligoclonal bands. Investigators involved in the revision have noted that adding the optic nerve is especially relevant because about one quarter of patients with MS present with ON first.

I could not verify the document's specific statement that MS can be diagnosed on dissemination in space alone when four of five topographies are involved, so I have left it out here. Consult the published criteria for the exact rules.

## Why estimates differ

- **Definition of the event.** Isolated ON, ON with other symptoms, and any visual symptom are different numerators.
- **Denominator.** Relapsing onset only, all MS, or a clinically isolated syndrome cohort give different rates. Progressive onset MS almost never starts with ON.
- **Ascertainment.** Tertiary centers, population registries, and clinic based series sample different patients. Recall of a first symptom decades earlier is imperfect.
- **Ancestry and diagnostic mix.** Higher antibody mediated disease in some populations changes what "ON first" means.
- **Era.** MRI and modern criteria diagnose MS earlier and capture milder presentations than cohorts assembled before the 2000s.


## Conclusion

The best supported statement is that ON is the first symptom in about 20 to 25% of relapsing onset MS, roughly 28% if any visual symptom is counted, about 2% in progressive onset disease, and lower with older onset. Single center studies such as Pleșa et al. fit within this range but cannot define it. The clinical weight of the finding lies in what follows: a first ON with brain MRI lesions carries a high chance of MS, and the 2024 McDonald revision now lets optic nerve evidence help confirm the diagnosis earlier.
